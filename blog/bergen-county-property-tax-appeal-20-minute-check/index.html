Bergen County Property Tax Appeal: How to Know in 20 Minutes If You Have a Case

By Tyler Sellers · August 6, 2026

Last updated August 2026.

I have been selling homes in Bergen County since 2019, with more than 30 closings across towns like Hackensack, Palisades Park, and Ridgefield Park. Property taxes come up in almost every one of those deals, and this week the county subreddit is full of the same two feelings: anger at the bill, and total fog about whether anything can be done. Almost everyone jumps straight to "hire a lawyer" or "leave New Jersey." Almost nobody runs the 20-minute check that tells you whether you even have a case.

Here is that check, with the actual state math behind it.

TL;DR

  • Divide your assessed value by your town's equalization ratio. That is what the town says your house is worth.
  • State law (Chapter 123) gives the town a 15% cushion. Divide the town's number by 1.15, compare it with your assessed value, and keep the bigger one. Homes like yours selling below that number means you may have a case.
  • Factual errors on your property record card are the fastest fix and often take one email.
  • Appeals can backfire on under-assessed homes, which is why you do the math before you file anything.

How do I know if my property taxes are too high?

Divide your assessed value by your town's equalization ratio. That single division tells you the market value your town is actually taxing you on, and it is the number almost nobody checks. Your assessed value is printed on your tax bill. The ratio takes one phone call: ask your assessor for this year's equalization ratio for your town.

An example with round numbers. Say your assessed value is $480,000 and your town's ratio is 0.92, which is roughly Fairview's certified number for 2026. Then $480,000 divided by 0.92 is about $521,700. The town is taxing you as if your house would sell for $521,700 today.

Now the only question that matters: would it? Not according to a Zillow Zestimate, and not according to asking prices down the street. According to what similar homes in your town have actually closed for. If the honest answer is "not even close," keep reading.

What is the equalization ratio in New Jersey?

The equalization ratio is the state's yearly measure of how far your town's assessments have drifted from real market prices. Assessments in most Bergen towns are not updated every year, but home prices move constantly, so the state publishes a correction factor per town, every year, based on actual sales.

Here is what that looks like in practice. I pulled a property record card in Park Ridge this week. The assessment has been exactly flat at about $325,000 for three straight years. The implied market value on that same record climbed from about $462,000 to about $507,000 over those three years, because the town's ratio fell from about 70% to 64.17%, the exact figure on the county's certified 2026 table. Nothing about the house changed. The market moved, and the ratio carried the assessment along with it. All figures rounded.

That is why "my assessment didn't go up" and "my taxes didn't go up" are two different sentences, and it connects to the bigger Bergen story: prices climbing on thin inventory pull every town's ratio down, year after year. The assessment is the town's opinion. The market has its own.

A low ratio is not a scandal. It usually means your town has not revalued in years while prices climbed, so the state corrects harder. What is Chapter 123, the 15% rule?

Chapter 123 is the 1973 state law that decides tax appeals with arithmetic, and it gives the town a cushion. Every assessment gets a tolerance corridor of 15% around the town's average ratio. Inside the corridor, you lose even if you are somewhat over-assessed. The law considers that close enough.

So the real test is this. Take the town's implied value of your home and divide it by 1.15. Then compare that answer with your assessed value, and keep whichever number is bigger. That bigger number is the bar. If homes like yours sell for less than the bar, you may have a case worth filing.

StepExample
Assessed value$480,000
Town's implied value ($480,000 divided by 0.92)$521,700
Implied value divided by 1.15$454,000
The bar (keep the bigger number)$480,000

Why does the assessed value win in this example? Because the law also caps the corridor at 100%: a town can never be entitled to tax you above your home's full value. I read the statute for this post, and most explanations online skip that cap entirely, which sends homeowners chasing the wrong target number. In towns with high ratios, the bar is simply your assessed value. In towns with lower ratios, the divide-by-1.15 number is the bar.

Can a property tax appeal backfire?

Yes, and this is the fear that keeps a lot of over-payers quiet, so let me be precise about it. The corridor cuts both ways. If the evidence at the hearing shows your home is worth so much more than your assessment that you fall below the corridor, the county board can raise your assessment, not lower it. That risk is real for people who file on anger.

It is close to zero for people who file on arithmetic. If you ran the math above and your home genuinely sells below the bar, you are on the right side of the corridor by construction. The math check is not homework for its own sake. It is the thing that makes the appeal safe.

What actually wins an appeal?

Closed sales, not feelings. Three to five recently sold homes in your town that genuinely resemble yours in size, age, style, and condition. Photographs of every condition issue an assessor's drive-by would never see. And one clean page: your assessed value, the ratio, the implied value, your comps, and the value you are requesting. County boards hear a lot of cases in a day, and the homeowner with one page and five real comps beats the homeowner with a folder and a grievance.

Before any of that, two free moves. Read your property record card line by line, because a wrong square footage, a bathroom you do not have, or a basement listed as finished is the fastest correction available and often takes one email to the assessor. And ask the assessor for an informal review before you file anything, because many clear errors get fixed in that conversation without a hearing.

When is the deadline to appeal in Bergen County?

The standard New Jersey deadline is April 1, and towns that completed a revaluation or reassessment that year run to May 1. There is no late filing. Miss the date and you wait a full year. If your town is on the 2026 revaluation list, your date and your assessment are both moving parts, so confirm this year's deadline in writing with your assessor either way.

The 20-minute version, start to finish

Find your assessed value on your tax bill. Call your assessor for this year's ratio. Divide the first number by the second to get the town's opinion of your home. Divide that by 1.15, compare with your assessed value, keep the bigger one. Then look at real closed sales and answer one question honestly: do homes like yours sell below the bar?

Common questions

How do I know if my property taxes are too high in NJ?

What is the equalization ratio in New Jersey?

It is the state's published measure of how a town's assessments compare to real market values, recalculated every year. An assessment of $480,000 in a town with a 0.92 ratio implies a market value of about $521,700.

What is Chapter 123 in a NJ tax appeal?

Chapter 123 is the state law that gives every assessment a tolerance corridor of 15% around the town's average ratio. Your appeal generally needs to show your home is worth less than the implied value divided by 1.15, or less than your assessed value, whichever is bigger.
