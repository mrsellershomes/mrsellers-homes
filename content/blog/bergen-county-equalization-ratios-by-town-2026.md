Every February and March, Bergen County homeowners open their tax bills and assessment postcards and go looking for one number their town makes strangely hard to find: the equalization ratio. It decides what market value your town is actually taxing you on, and most town websites bury it in meeting minutes. So here is the whole county in one table, from the certified source, with instructions for using it.

**TL;DR**

- The equalization ratio converts your assessed value into the market value your town is taxing you on. Divide assessed value by the ratio as a decimal.
- Bergen County's 2026 ratios run from 50.76 in Garfield to 128.09 in Rockleigh. Your neighbor's tax logic does not transfer across town lines.
- Source: the Final Equalization Table certified by the Bergen County Board of Taxation on May 6, 2026, [downloadable here](/downloads/2026-bergen-equalization-table.pdf).

## What is the equalization ratio?

It is the state's yearly correction factor between assessed values and real market prices, published per town. Most Bergen towns do not update assessments every year, but prices move constantly, so the ratio closes the gap. An assessment of $480,000 in a town with a ratio of 92 implies a market value of about $521,700. The full walk-through, including New Jersey's 15% rule for deciding whether an appeal is worth filing, is in the [Bergen County property tax appeal 20-minute check](/blog/bergen-county-property-tax-appeal-20-minute-check/).

To use the table below: the ratios are percentages, so slide the decimal two places before dividing. A ratio of 97.15 becomes 0.9715, and your implied market value is your assessed value divided by 0.9715.

## How to read this table

A low ratio is not a scandal. It usually means the town has not revalued in years while prices climbed, so the state corrects harder. Garfield at 50.76 is taxing on assessments that average about half of current market value, which is exactly why a $300,000 assessment there does not mean a $300,000 house.

A ratio above 100 means assessments in that town sit above market overall, which usually follows a recent revaluation or reassessment. If your town revalued this year, your appeal deadline moves too, which is covered in the [Bergen County property tax revaluations for 2026](/blog/bergen-county-property-tax-revaluations-2026/) rundown.

One precision note: this certified county table is the right number for screening your own assessment. If you get as far as filing an appeal, your assessor confirms the exact Chapter 123 average ratio for your town, which can differ slightly from the county equalization figure.

## How to use your town's ratio

Three steps, about 20 minutes. Find your assessed value on your property tax bill. Divide it by your town's ratio from the table, as a decimal. Then compare the answer to what homes like yours actually sell for, using closed sales, not online estimates.
