*Last updated July 2026.*

I sell homes in Bergen County, more than 30 closings on both the buyer and seller side since I got licensed in 2019, and I run one rule that surprises almost every buyer I work with: scan every lot for an underground oil tank before closing. Every lot. Old house, new construction, vacant land that never held a house. The scan costs about $300, and it is the cheapest insurance in NJ real estate.

Bergen is tank country, and the reason is age. A huge share of our housing stock went up before 1975, much of it built with oil heat, and when those houses converted to gas the buried tank did not always come out. But the build year only explains why tanks are everywhere. It does not tell you which lot is clean, and that is why my rule is every lot, not just the old ones.

Almost everything ranking for this topic is written by tank-removal companies or law firms. Both have something to sell you. My lane is different, and it is the whole bet of this blog: be the Bergen realtor who checks the primary sources while the internet stays confidently wrong. I checked every claim on this page against the official pages of the New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection (NJDEP), the state insurance department's consumer guidance, and the current mandatory seller disclosure form. I flag every number that comes from contractor quotes instead of an official government source.

Quick disclaimer: not legal advice. Tank issues sit at the intersection of contract language and environmental rules. Confirm your specific situation with your attorney.

**TL;DR:**

- Scan every NJ lot you buy for a tank, regardless of build year, vacant land included. A sweep typically runs $250 to $400.
- A clean removal typically quotes $1,500 to $4,500. A leak commonly starts at $5,000 to $15,000, and groundwater cases can pass $100,000.
- Budget as if every dollar is yours. Contractors still advertise a state fund that pays for tank work, but it closed to non-leaking tanks back in May 2011.
- A sand-filled tank with no soil report resolves nothing. NJDEP's own FAQ says contamination turns up under properly abandoned tanks all the time.

## Should I buy a house with an underground oil tank in NJ?

Yes, if the tank is resolved before closing. That means removed by an NJDEP closure-certified contractor with clean soil results, or a past leak remediated with an NFA (No Further Action) letter you can verify yourself for free. An unresolved tank is a negotiation item, not a reason to run.

Here is why you cannot just ignore it. Most NJ homeowners insurance policies exclude oil-tank pollution liability. The NJ Department of Banking and Insurance says so on its own consumer page, and it notes that many carriers have internal rules that can stop them from insuring a home with an oil tank at all. Insurers that do cover it typically offer a one-time $100,000 liability buy-back, and if you decline it once, it is gone on future policies. Lenders are similar. No bank publishes an oil-tank policy, but many decline or hesitate on homes with an in-ground or undocumented tank.

So the tank question is really a financing and insurance question. Resolve it before closing and it disappears. Inherit it and it becomes your problem the day you try to sell.

## What is a tank sweep, and how much does it cost in NJ?

A tank sweep is a scan of the property for buried tanks. A technician walks the yard with metal-detection and ground-scanning equipment, checks the basement for old supply lines and fill pipes, and gives you a written report. Expect quotes of $200 to $500, with most residential sweeps landing between $250 and $400.

Timing matters more than price. Order the sweep immediately after attorney review concludes, inside your inspection window, alongside the regular home inspection. If you are new to NJ contracts, I explain [how attorney review works in New Jersey](https://mrsellers.homes/blog/nj-attorney-review-explained/) in its own post. The sequence is simple: sign, clear attorney review, sweep within days. A tank found on day 3 of your inspection window is leverage. A tank found after closing is your bill.

Sweep every lot, not just old houses. That is my standing practice with my own buyers: every purchase gets scanned, including new construction and vacant land that never held a house. Houses get demolished, tanks stay buried, and paper records that old are spotty. A gas conversion decades ago, a knockdown rebuild, an empty lot: any of them can leave steel in the ground with no paper trail. The build year explains why tanks are common in Bergen. It does not tell you which lot is safe, and no removal company or law firm writing about this will hand you that rule.

## How much does oil tank removal cost in NJ, and who pays?

Contractors typically quote $1,500 to $4,500 all-in for a standard residential tank with clean soil: the dig, the township permit, backfill, and soil samples. Reported permit fees run $50 to $500 depending on the town, and the township sends an inspector to witness the pull. One rule with no exceptions: only an NJDEP closure-certified contractor can legally remove an underground heating oil tank in New Jersey.

Here is the full cost ladder, from best case to worst. Nobody else publishes it this way, because every site ranking for these searches sells one rung of it.

| Scenario | What you are paying for | Typical 2026 range |
|---|---|---|
| Tank sweep | Locate a buried tank, or rule one out | $200 to $500, most quotes $250 to $400 |
| Clean removal | Dig, permit, inspection, backfill, soil samples | $1,500 to $4,500 |
| Minor contamination | Extra soil dug out and hauled away | Add roughly $3,000 to $8,000 |
| Typical leak cleanup | Soil remediation to state standard | $5,000 to $15,000, most commonly quoted $8,000 to $10,000 |
| Major excavation | A larger contaminated area | $15,000 to $25,000 and up |
| Groundwater involvement | Cleanup reaching the water table or a neighboring lot | $100,000 plus, occasionally several hundred thousand |

Everything below the sweep line is a contractor-quoted range, not an official schedule, so treat those as planning numbers. The shape is what matters: each rung down multiplies the cost, and a $300 sweep is what keeps you off the ladder entirely.

Timeline: plan on 1 to 3 weeks for a clean removal, by contractors' own reported timelines. The dig itself takes 1 to 2 days, and permit lead time eats the rest. Contamination stretches the job to weeks or months. Build this into your closing date early, not the week before.

Who pays? Nothing in NJ law forces a seller to remove a tank or bars selling a house with one. It is negotiated. Sellers usually end up paying for removal before closing, and the reason is simple: if this buyer walks over the tank, the next buyer will ask for the exact same thing. Refusing does not make the tank go away. It re-runs the standoff with a later buyer, on a house the market now knows has a tank. Some deals instead close with money held aside in escrow until the tank work is finished.

If you are the seller, this cost lands on top of your normal [Bergen County seller closing costs breakdown](https://mrsellers.homes/blog/bergen-county-seller-closing-costs-2026/). Sweep and resolve before you list, and you sell a clean house instead of negotiating a compromised one.

## What happens if the oil tank leaked?

Report a discovered leak to NJDEP immediately. The hotline is 1-877-WARNDEP, NJDEP's own FAQ says the report is due within 15 minutes of discovery, and closure-certified contractors make that call as a matter of course. Understand what that means: once the tank comes out leaking, there is no quiet option.

From there the cleanup runs under New Jersey's Heating Oil Tank System Remediation Rules (N.J.A.C. 7:26F). An NJDEP-certified firm remediates the soil, files a Remedial Action Report with a $400 state review fee, and NJDEP reviews it, typically within a few weeks. The finish line is the NFA letter, covered below.

Cost-wise, you are now on the lower rungs of the table above. The genuinely good news: most residential leaks land in the $5,000 to $15,000 band by contractors' reported numbers, not the six-figure horror stories. The six-figure cases are real, and they are almost always groundwater cases. Handle the tank early, and that worst case never gets the chance to be yours.

## Will the state pay for oil tank removal in NJ?

No, not in any way you should plan on. This is the biggest myth in the entire topic, and it is still being advertised.

Here is the actual status of New Jersey's UST (underground storage tank) Fund, straight from NJDEP's own page, updated June 2026. I am quoting the state, not a contractor:

- Applications for non-leaking tanks have been closed since May 3, 2011, due to insufficient funds. That is fifteen years. If your tank never leaked, there is no state money. Period.
- Applications for leaking residential tanks are still accepted, but NJDEP says they "shall not be reviewed or processed at this time." They get date-stamped and queued for whenever funding appears, with a stated one-year wait on all new applications.
- NJDEP posts its own warning: contractors advertise free state funding, but "the UST Fund has never guaranteed funding for applicants to this program."

Read that last one again. The state environmental agency published a warning about how this program gets marketed. If anyone pitching you tank work mentions state money, ask them to pull up the NJDEP UST Fund page on their phone. Then plan on paying for the work out of your own pocket, because the state almost certainly will not.

## Is a sand-filled or abandoned-in-place oil tank safe?

No, not proven safe, and that distinction costs people real money. A tank filled with sand or foam under the old construction-code process was legally decommissioned. That is a statement about paperwork, not about soil.

NJDEP's own FAQ acknowledges there are many situations where a tank abandoned in place in full accordance with the NJ Uniform Construction Code is later removed and contamination is discovered underneath. The reason is mechanical: a buried tank cannot be pumped completely dry, and it can leak before, during, or after the filling. Sand on the inside says nothing about oil on the outside.

So treat "the tank was decommissioned" as the start of the conversation, not the end. The real questions: was the soil tested, and where is the report? No soil report means nothing is resolved, and many lenders will not approve the deal without proof of proper closure. Selling a house with a filled tank? Get soil testing done before you list. Buying one? Price the uncertainty, or make the seller close it out.

## What is an NFA letter, and do I need one?

An NFA (No Further Action) letter is NJDEP's closure document for a leak. It states that a discharge from the tank was remediated to state standard. If the house had a leak, the NFA is exactly the document you want, and you can verify it yourself in about two minutes.

Now the nuance almost every article misses: a tank that never leaked has no NFA letter, and never will. NJDEP's FAQ is explicit that when a tank is closed with no evidence of a discharge and passes the municipal inspection, no NFA letter is needed or issued. Demanding an NFA on a clean removal signals you do not know the process. Here is the document set that actually fits each case:

- Tank removed, soil clean: the municipal closure permit or certificate of approval, plus the soil sample report. That is the complete set.
- Tank leaked and was remediated: the paperwork above, plus the NFA letter.
- Tank abandoned in place: the closure permit plus a soil report. No soil report, treat it as unresolved.

Use NJDEP's free DataMiner tool to verify an NFA is real: run the report called "UHOT NFA Letters Search by Address" (UHOT is the state's shorthand for an unregulated heating oil tank). No account, no fee, no taking the seller's word for anything. Nobody ranking for these searches teaches the DataMiner lookup, and it is the easiest piece of due diligence in this whole subject.

One caution: an NFA covers the discharge that was remediated. Do not read it as a promise that no second tank exists somewhere else on the property. A sweep still earns its $300 even on a house with clean paperwork.

## What if the seller won't deal with the oil tank?

Then you decide what the house is worth with the tank still in it, because that is what you would be buying. No law forces the seller's hand, but the leverage math mostly favors the buyer. Every future buyer will find the same tank, ask the same question, and demand the same fix. A seller who refuses is usually not saving the removal cost. They are deferring it, plus a discount, onto a smaller pool of buyers who can even get the deal financed.

The disclosure rules also changed recently, and most articles ranking for this topic predate the change. Every residential seller in New Jersey must now complete the Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement before the buyer becomes obligated under the contract, under the Real Estate Consumer Protection Enhancement Act (P.L. 2024, c.32), effective August 1, 2024. The form asks about tanks directly: whether the seller is aware of any underground storage tank now or previously on the property, and whether any tank has been tested, with test reports or closure certificates attached. "Nobody asked" is no longer a thing. Real estate attorneys also warn that sellers who conceal a known tank can face liability after closing. That conversation belongs with your lawyer, but the direction of the risk is obvious.

Your protection as a buyer runs through your contract, and getting it right is a team effort. Your buyer's agent guides the whole play: how to handle the tank, what to ask your attorney for, and the communication with the seller's side to make sure the seller is the one paying for the work. Your attorney then writes that protection into the contract during attorney review, so it is spelled out exactly what happens if a tank turns up. Put the sweep inside your inspection window, then let your agent and your attorney do their jobs.
