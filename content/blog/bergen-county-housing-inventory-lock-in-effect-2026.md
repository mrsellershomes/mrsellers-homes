*Last updated July 2026.*

I sell homes in Bergen County, more than 30 closings on both the buyer and the seller side since I got licensed in 2019, and the question I get most right now is not about price. It is why there is nothing to look at.

Here is the answer in one number. In June 2026 Bergen County had 1,741 homes on the market. In June 2019 it had 5,347. That is 67.4% fewer houses for sale than the last normal year, in a county of roughly 979,000 people.

My bet with this blog is that housing is an economics story before it is a real estate story, and that a Bergen agent who reads the data beats a Bergen agent who repeats headlines. This post is that bet in one place. Every figure below is dated and sourced, and where I am working from a single source or someone else's reporting instead of a document I read myself, I say so on the spot.

Start with the thing the national headlines have completely wrong. Search this topic and you will hit a wall of 2026 posts announcing that the mortgage rate lock-in effect is finally breaking. I went to the underlying government data. It is not breaking. In the first quarter of 2026 it barely moved at all, the slowest erosion since the whole thing started in 2022.

**TL;DR:**

- **The same Bergen household that pays $2,192 a month on a house they bought in 2021 would pay $4,824 for the median house today.** Same county, same commute, 120% more money. That is the whole story in one line.
- Bergen County has 67.4% fewer homes for sale than in June 2019. Single-family houses alone are down 72.8%.
- The lock-in effect did not break in 2026, it stalled. Roughly 78% of US homeowners with a mortgage pay under 6%, and just under half pay under 4%, while the market rate sits at 6.58%.
- "Inventory is recovering" is a condo story. Bergen condo and townhouse inventory rose 35.6% year over year while single-family inventory fell 3.5%.
- Waiting for rates to drop cuts both ways. A one-point drop hands every buyer about 11% more purchasing power at once, into a market with 2.5 months of supply.

## Why is there no housing inventory in Bergen County?

Three forces stack on top of each other, and only one of them is about mortgage rates.

**One, the mortgage people already have is too good to give up.** Most homeowners here are sitting on a loan far cheaper than anything they could get today, so moving costs them money every month for the rest of the next loan.

**Two, almost nothing new gets built.** Bergen approved 2,228 housing units in all of 2025, which is 0.60% of the county's existing housing stock.

**Three, the owners who do want to move refuse to list first.** They will not put their house up until they have already bought the next one, which keeps their current house off the market while they lose out on the ones that would replace it.

Here is what that looks like on the ground, from New Jersey REALTORS' own county reports:

| Bergen County, June | 2019 | 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Homes for sale, all types | 5,347 | 1,741 | down 67.4% |
| Single-family homes for sale | 3,926 | 1,066 | down 72.8% |
| Single-family months supply | 6.5 | 2.5 | down 62% |
| Single-family days on market | 52 | 28 | down 46% |
| Percent of list price received | 98.7% | 105.5% | up 6.8 points |

Percentage changes are rounded to one decimal place, and the months-supply and days-on-market changes to the nearest whole percent.

Months supply is the plain-English version of all of this: how long it would take to sell every house currently listed, at the current pace, if nothing new came on. Six months is the traditional dividing line between a buyer's market and a seller's market. Bergen single-family sits at 2.5 months. In 2019 it sat at 6.5.

The price line moved to match. The median Bergen County single-family home sold for $946,000 in June 2026, up 7.3% from a year earlier.

## What is the mortgage rate lock-in effect?

The lock-in effect is what happens when the mortgage you already have is worth more than the house you want next. If you borrowed at 3% and today's rate is 6.58%, selling means voluntarily doubling your interest cost on a bigger loan. Most people decline.

The scale of it is the part people underestimate. Per the FHFA (Federal Housing Finance Agency) and its National Mortgage Database, as of the first quarter of 2026:

| Rate on the mortgage they already have | Share of US borrowers, Q1 2026 |
|---|---|
| Under 3% | 19.5% |
| Under 4% | 49.9% |
| Under 5% | 66.7% |
| Under 6% | 77.9% |
| 6% or higher | 22.1% |

Each row is cumulative, so "under 5%" includes everybody in the rows above it.

Read the bottom two rows together. Roughly 78% of American homeowners with a mortgage are paying less than 6%, and the market rate on July 23, 2026 was 6.58% on Freddie Mac's weekly survey. Daily lender pricing was running closer to 6.85% that same day per the Mortgage News Daily index, a one-year high. Just under half of all borrowers are under 4%.

A sourcing note, because it matters here. Those share figures come from one government dataset, read independently by several analysts, not from several independent datasets. It is a strong source. It is still a single source, and I would rather tell you that than pretend I triangulated it.

There is a behavioral fingerprint too. ICE Mortgage Technology reported in its July 2026 Mortgage Monitor that roughly 3.9 million borrowers who took out a mortgage between 2020 and 2022 have since added a second loan against the house instead of refinancing or selling. That number reaches me through ICE's press materials and trade coverage rather than a document I could open myself, so treat it as color and not as a load-bearing fact. The direction is still unmistakable. People are borrowing against the cheap mortgage rather than giving it up.

## Is the lock-in effect ending in 2026?

No. It slowed almost to a stop.

The share of US mortgages under 4% fell 20 basis points in the first quarter of 2026. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so that is a decline of two tenths of one percent over three months, and it is the smallest quarterly drop since the share started falling in 2022. Two independent analysts who follow this dataset closely, Calculated Risk and Wolf Street, both flagged the stall.

That matters because the lock-in effect is not unwinding for the reason the headlines imply. Nobody is deciding that a 3% mortgage was overrated. The share falls because old loans age out. People die, divorce, relocate for a job, pay the loan off, or get forced into a move they did not plan. It is arithmetic, not a change of heart. And in early 2026 even the arithmetic stalled, because rates went back up instead of down.

So when you read that buyers are finally getting more choices in 2026, know what is not behind it. There is no wave of homeowners deciding to sell.

## How do mortgage rates affect home prices in NJ?

Rates decide what a monthly payment buys, and that is the entire mechanism. At Bergen County's median single-family price, one percentage point on the rate is worth about $500 a month. Half a point is about $250.

Here is the math on the June 2026 median of $946,000 with 20% down, which is a $756,800 loan. These figures are principal and interest only, with no property taxes and no homeowners insurance included. In Bergen County that is a meaningful omission and I would rather flag it than bury it, because taxes are a large share of a real payment here and they are changing in a lot of towns right now. I cover that separately in my breakdown of the [Bergen County 2026 property tax revaluations](https://mrsellers.homes/blog/bergen-county-property-tax-revaluations-2026/).

| Mortgage rate | Monthly principal and interest | Difference vs today |
|---|---|---|
| 3.00% | $3,191 | $1,633 less |
| 5.58% | $4,335 | $489 less |
| 6.00% | $4,538 | $286 less |
| 6.58% (Freddie Mac, July 23, 2026) | $4,824 | today's number |
| 7.00% | $5,035 | $211 more |
| 7.58% | $5,332 | $508 more |

Payments are rounded to the nearest dollar, on a standard 30-year fixed loan.

Now put a real Bergen household inside that table. Somebody who bought at $650,000 in 2021 with 20% down is carrying a $520,000 loan at 3%. Their payment is $2,192 a month.

Say they want more space and want to stay in the county. They buy the June 2026 median single-family house at $946,000, put 20% down, and finance $756,800 at 6.58%. That payment is $4,824 a month.

**Same county. Same commute. The payment goes up $2,632 a month, which is 120% more, for a house that is bigger but nowhere near twice the house.**

That is why they are not listing. It is not sentiment and it is not stubbornness. It is arithmetic, and it pays them every single month they stay put.

## Is Bergen County inventory recovering in 2026?

Only if you are shopping for a condo.

This is the most misleading thing in the current coverage, and it took ten minutes with the county report to see it. Bergen's inventory recovery is entirely attached housing.

| Bergen County, June 2026 | Single-family | Townhouse and condo |
|---|---|---|
| Homes for sale | 1,066 | 655 |
| Change vs June 2025 | down 3.5% | up 35.6% |
| Months supply | 2.5 | 3.7 |
| Change in months supply | down 3.8% | up 42.3% |
| Median sale price | $946,000 | $575,000 |
| Days on market | 28 | 39 |

Every headline about Bergen buyers finally getting choices in 2026 is describing the second column. The first column, which is what people mean when they say "a house," got tighter over the same twelve months. If you are shopping for a single-family home in Bergen County, you are competing in a market with less supply than you had a year ago, not more.

I have not seen anyone else split this out, and it is not hidden. It is on the front page of the New Jersey REALTORS county report. It just changes the entire story, so nobody repeating a national headline bothers to look.

## Will new construction fix Bergen County's housing shortage?

No, and the gap is not close.

New Jersey's Department of Community Affairs publishes every building permit issued in the state. Bergen County approved 2,228 housing units in all of 2025. Of those, 1,282 were one-and-two-family homes, which is to say houses.

Hold that next to demand. In the first six months of 2026 alone, 2,219 single-family homes sold in Bergen County. A full year of approved house construction covers well under half of six months of house turnover.

Zoom out and it gets starker. Bergen has 370,477 housing units and 978,641 residents per US Census Bureau estimates. The 2,228 units approved in 2025 equal 0.60% of the county's existing housing stock, which is one new unit for every 166 that already exist. And the county approved fewer units in 2025 than in 2019, 2,228 against 3,017, a 26% drop, over the same stretch in which inventory fell 67%.

Two honest caveats on those permit numbers. Approved is not built, so a permit count is a ceiling and not a delivery. And the 2025 figure comes from the December year-to-date report, where a handful of municipalities filed no data, so the true number is a little higher than 2,228.

The reason nothing changes is not mysterious. Bergen is 70 separate municipalities, each running its own zoning and its own approvals, on a county that is largely built out already. No lever exists that anyone can pull to add thousands of houses here in a year. The supply of Bergen County houses is, functionally, the houses that already exist.

## Why won't homeowners sell if prices are this high?

Because selling into this market means becoming a buyer in it. That is the trap, and it does not require an interest rate to work.

I am working with a household right now that owns a house in this market. I would list it the day they tell me to go. They have one standing rule: they will not sell until they have found the house they are moving into. They do not need to buy first for financing reasons. It is a choice, and it is a completely reasonable one, because nobody wants to sell the roof over their head and hope.

So they shop. They find something. They get outbid. They shop again.

Meanwhile a house that would be on the market tomorrow stays off it. Their listing is being held hostage by the same shortage that keeps beating them. Multiply that by every move-up owner in the county running the same rule and you get a shortage that feeds itself. The fewer houses there are, the harder it is to buy one. The harder it is to buy one, the fewer people are willing to sell theirs.

Notice what that story does not contain. No interest rate appears anywhere in it. The lock-in effect is real and it is the largest force here, and it is still not the only thing keeping Bergen houses off the market.

One more read from my own pipeline, and I want to be precise about what it is and what it is not. Across my own deals that died over the past few years, getting outbid on price is the single most common reason by far. Not inspection problems. Not financing. Not cold feet. Price. Those deals sit across North Jersey and include towns outside Bergen County, so I am calling this a North Jersey pattern and not a Bergen County statistic, and a few of those lost-deal reasons are my own reconstruction rather than something a buyer put in writing. Directionally though, it is not close.

## Should I wait for mortgage rates to drop before buying in Bergen County?

Know exactly what you are waiting for before you decide.

Run the math backwards. At 6.58%, a $4,824 monthly payment supports a $756,800 loan. If the rate fell a full point to 5.58%, that same $4,824 payment would support $842,100, which is about 11% more house. At 20% down, that is a $1,052,600 purchase price instead of $946,000.

Now apply that to every other buyer at the same moment. A one-point drop in rates hands every buyer in Bergen County roughly 11% more purchasing power on the same day, into a market with 1,066 single-family homes for sale and 2.5 months of supply. Falling rates do not build houses. They add bidders.

The rate cut you are waiting for is the thing that bids against you.

That is not a prediction, and it is not a reason to rush. Rates just hit a one-year high, which is exactly why I will not tell you where they go next. The point holds either direction. If rates rise, your payment on the same house gets worse. If rates fall, your competition gets stronger and prices absorb the savings. Neither path produces more houses for sale in Bergen County, because the constraint here was never financing. It is the 1,066 listings.

## The bottom line on Bergen County's housing shortage

Almost everyone writing about this market is selling you a direction. Rates are about to fall. Prices are about to crack. It is finally a buyer's market. I sell houses in Bergen County, so the only thing I need from you is an accurate picture, because a client who buys or sells on a wrong assumption is a client I lose.

Here is my accurate picture as of July 2026. The lock-in effect did not break, it stalled. Bergen's inventory recovery is a condo story, and the single-family market got tighter over the same year. New construction is replacing a fraction of what sells. And a real share of the houses that should be listed are sitting behind owners who cannot buy their next one.
