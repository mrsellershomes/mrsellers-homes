Those records get basic facts wrong all the time, including how big the house is and how many bedrooms it has.

Here is the short version. An automated home value estimate reads public records, public records are frequently wrong, and they are wrong in ways that are invisible to a model and obvious to anyone standing in the house.

## Are Zillow Zestimates accurate in Bergen County, NJ?

No, not accurately enough to price a house on. A Zestimate is an AVM (automated valuation model), which is software that estimates a home's value from public records, past sales, and recent sales nearby. It has never been inside your house, it has no idea what condition it is in, and the public records it runs on are frequently wrong.

That last part is what most articles skip. The usual complaint about these tools is that they cannot see your renovated kitchen, which is true and which Zillow itself will tell you.

**The bigger problem is one layer down.** The official description of your house that every one of these tools starts from can be wrong about how big your house is and how many bedrooms it has.

None of this is a shot at Zillow. The model works with the information it has. The problem is how little of that information there is, and how much of it is wrong.

## How does a Zestimate actually work?

It reads public records and it reads recent sales near you. That is the whole method.

The inputs are your town's property record card, which is the assessor's official description of your house, plus your deed and sale history, your tax assessment, and the recent sales around you. When a home is actively listed, the listing details feed in too. Then the model weights all of that and produces a number.

Notice what is not on that list. Nobody opens your front door. Whatever the records say your house is, that is the house the model prices.

## Why is my Zestimate wrong?

Start with the document the estimate is built on. Your town keeps a property record card for your house: square footage, room counts, year built, style, and a sketch of the footprint. Every automated estimate in existence is reading that card. Most homeowners have never seen theirs.

Here is one from a Bergen County split-level I know well.

The town's card lists about 3,400 square feet of living area and five bedrooms. A professionally measured floor plan of the same house came back at roughly 2,800 square feet above grade and six bedrooms. And the card leaves out, entirely and on purpose, a finished lower level of about 1,500 square feet with its own kitchen, a full bathroom, two more bedrooms, and a walk-out to the yard.

Put those two side by side. The official record overstates the above-grade space by about 600 square feet and understates the actual finished living space by close to 1,000 square feet. At the same time, in opposite directions.

Neither error is fraud and neither one is unusual.

**The over-count is old math that nobody ever checked.** The card was drawn decades ago on a one-and-a-half story footprint, the upper half story got counted generously, and nobody has re-measured the house since the 1950s.

**The under-count is a rule rather than a mistake.** Standard measurement practice counts finished below-grade space separately from above-grade living area, so a legal, finished, walk-out lower level with a full kitchen in it does not show up in the square footage at all.

An appraiser standing in that house resolves both of those in ten minutes. A model resolves neither, because a model has no way to know the card is wrong. **It will confidently price a house that does not exist.**

## What can an online home value estimate not see?

Almost everything that decides the price.

| What the model can read | What actually moves the number |
|---|---|
| Square footage on the town card | Whether that square footage was ever measured or inherited from the 1950s |
| Bedroom and bathroom counts | Whether the layout works or you walk through one bedroom to reach another |
| Year built | Whether the kitchen and baths were redone last year or in 1994 |
| Recent sales nearby | Whether those houses are anything like yours |
| Lot size in acres | Frontage, grade, and whether two cars fit in the driveway |
| Permit history, where the town has digitized it | Renovations that never got a permit, and permits that never updated the card |

Two houses on the same Bergen street with identical bed and bath counts can be a hundred thousand dollars apart on condition and layout alone. On paper they are the same house, so a national model prices them the same. That is not the software making a mistake. That is the software doing exactly what it was built to do with the only information it has.

## Why do online estimates get whole streets wrong?

Because they average the block, and some houses are not average.

A Bergen County rowhouse I know went on the market at $740,000. Pre-World War I, on a lot 25 feet wide. It cut to $679,000 and expired about five and a half months later with zero offers at either price. It later rented for $4,000 a month. On my read, that rent implies a value somewhere between $50,000 and $100,000 below even the reduced asking price.

A narrow lot and an attached house are exactly the details an automated model averages away. It sees a house on a street where houses sell for a certain number. It does not care that this one is 25 feet wide, attached to its neighbors, and a century older than the houses it is being compared to. Buyers saw all of that at the first showing. It took five and a half months and two price cuts for the asking price to catch up to what buyers knew on day one.

## What do most sellers actually price their house off of?

The house down the street. Not a website.

This is the part I promised at the top. I went back through my own notes on every client I have worked with, and I cannot find a single seller who ever quoted me a Zestimate, a Redfin Estimate, or any online number. Not one.

**What I find instead, over and over, is a specific neighbor's sale.** The colonial on the corner got $850,000. That becomes the floor, and it is a reasonable place to start.

Here is the problem with that floor, and it is not a problem with you. **The neighbor's sale price has the same blind spots the algorithm has.** Nobody checked whether that colonial had a finished lower level, a redone kitchen, or 15 more feet of frontage than your house. It is one sale, on one house, and that house is not yours.

So the real problem is not that people trust computers too much. It is that the software and the seller both price off the house down the street, and the house down the street says very little about yours.

Not close, and not in a predictable direction. Twenty-two of my closings have both the original list price and the final sale price on record. Here is the spread.

It is North Jersey, not Bergen County. The median is the number to hold onto. "Up to 13.4% over list" sounds better, but it tells you less. ## How do I fix a wrong Zestimate?

Fix the facts at the source. Three steps, in order.

- **Step one, pull your property record card.** Your town assessor's office has it, and many Bergen towns post cards online. Check the square footage, the bedroom and bathroom count, the year built, and whether your finished lower level appears anywhere at all.
- **Step two, update your home facts on the sites themselves.** Most home value sites let the verified owner correct beds, baths, square footage, and renovation history. It takes about ten minutes and it feeds the model directly.
- **Step three, fix the card itself through the assessor, carefully.** Raising the recorded living area on your card can raise your assessment, which raises your tax bill. Have that conversation with your assessor before you correct anything upward. The opposite case, a record card that overstates your house, is one of the fastest ways to win a correction, which I walk through in the [Bergen County property tax appeal 20-minute check](/blog/bergen-county-property-tax-appeal-20-minute-check/). A lot of Bergen towns are already in motion on this, which I cover in my breakdown of the [Bergen County 2026 property tax revaluations](https://mrsellers.homes/blog/bergen-county-property-tax-revaluations-2026/).

Then price off actual comparable sales that a person has physically been inside, not off a number that came out of a card nobody has re-measured in decades.

## The bottom line on online home value estimates

The model is not the problem. The paperwork it reads is the problem, and nobody is going to fix your paperwork for you.

Your Zestimate is a fine place to start a conversation and a terrible place to end one. It is built on a town record card that may overstate your house, understate your house, or do both at once, and the model has no way to find out. Everything that makes your house worth more or less than the one down the street is inside it, and no model has ever been inside.
